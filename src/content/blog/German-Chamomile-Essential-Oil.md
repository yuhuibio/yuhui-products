# German Chamomile Essential Oil: Benefits, Chamazulene & GC-MS Profile

German Chamomile Essential Oil is widely recognized for its distinctive deep blue color, characteristic aroma, and unique chemical composition. Unlike many other essential oils, its blue appearance is closely linked to a compound formed during the steam distillation process: **chamazulene**.

But color is only one part of the story.

For aromatherapy enthusiasts, German Chamomile is often associated with skin care and soothing formulations. For essential oil buyers and formulators, its chemical profile, botanical identity, and batch consistency are equally important.

In this guide, we explore what makes German Chamomile Essential Oil different, why it turns blue, how Egyptian and Hungarian chemotypes compare, and what buyers should look for when evaluating its quality.

---

## 1. What Is German Chamomile Essential Oil?

German Chamomile (*Matricaria chamomilla L.*) is an annual herb belonging to the Asteraceae family. It is also commonly known by the botanical synonym *Matricaria recutita L.*

![German chamomile flower (Matricaria chamomilla)](/images/german-chamomile-flower.jpg)

The plant is easily recognized by its white ray florets surrounding a yellow central disc. As the flower develops, its receptacle becomes increasingly conical and hollow.

German Chamomile Essential Oil is generally obtained by steam distillation of the flower heads or flowering tops.

One of its most distinctive characteristics is its blue to blue-green color, which makes it stand out from many other essential oils.

### German Chamomile vs. Roman Chamomile

German Chamomile and Roman Chamomile are often confused because they share the common name "chamomile." However, they are two different botanical species with different chemical profiles.

| Characteristic | German Chamomile | Roman Chamomile |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Botanical name** | *Matricaria chamomilla L.* | *Chamaemelum nobile* |
| **Plant type** | Annual herb | Perennial herb |
| **Main chemical characteristics** | Chamazulene, α-bisabolol and its oxides, β-farnesene | Mainly ester compounds |
| **Typical oil color** | Blue to blue-green | Pale yellow to yellowish |
| **Aroma** | Herbaceous, warm, slightly sweet | Sweet, soft, often apple-like |

Although both oils are used in aromatherapy and personal care formulations, they should not be treated as interchangeable ingredients.

German Chamomile is particularly interesting for formulators looking at blue essential oils and bisabolol-related chemical profiles, while Roman Chamomile is often selected for its characteristic soft, fruity aroma.

## 2. Why Is German Chamomile Essential Oil Blue?

The answer is **chamazulene**.

![German chamomile flowering field](/images/german-chamomile-test.jpg)

Chamazulene is a blue-colored sesquiterpene hydrocarbon responsible for much of the characteristic appearance of German Chamomile Essential Oil.

However, there is an interesting detail behind its formation: fresh German Chamomile flowers do not contain large amounts of ready-made chamazulene.

Instead, they contain a precursor called **matricin**.

During steam distillation, heat causes matricin to undergo chemical transformation, resulting in the formation of chamazulene. This process gives the distilled essential oil its characteristic blue or blue-green appearance.

In other words, the blue color is not simply extracted from the naturally white and yellow flowers. It develops through chemical changes during processing.

This also helps explain why German Chamomile oils can vary in color. Factors that may influence the final appearance include:

- The natural chemical composition of the plant material.
- Genetic and chemotypic differences.
- Harvesting stage and raw material condition.
- Distillation temperature and processing conditions.

However, a darker blue color does not automatically mean better quality. The actual chemical composition should always be considered alongside the appearance of the oil.

## 3. German Chamomile Chemotypes: Egyptian vs. Hungarian

One of the most important things to understand about German Chamomile Essential Oil is that it can have different chemical composition profiles.

When comparing GC-MS reports from different batches, buyers may notice considerable differences in chamazulene, α-bisabolol, and bisabolol oxides.

These variations do not necessarily indicate poor quality or adulteration. They may reflect differences in the natural chemical type of the plant and its growing and processing conditions.

**ISO 19332:2020**, the international standard for German Chamomile Essential Oil, specifies two commercial types:

- Egyptian type
- Hungarian type

The following table summarizes the characteristic chromatographic ranges specified in the standard.

### ISO 19332:2020 – Chromatographic Profile

| Major constituent | Egyptian type | Hungarian type |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **trans-β-Farnesene** | 15–35% | 20–51% |
| **α-Bisabolol** | 1–10% | 15–40% |
| **α-Bisabolol oxide A** | 35–50% | 2–27% |
| **α-Bisabolol oxide B** | 2–8% | 2–21% |
| **Chamazulene** | 2–5% | 5–22% |
| **α-Bisabolone oxide A** | 2–6.5% | 1–4% |

> *Source: ISO 19332:2020. Values represent the specified chromatographic composition ranges, not the guaranteed composition of every commercial batch.*

The differences are quite noticeable.

**Egyptian type** — Egyptian German Chamomile is characterized by a relatively high proportion of α-bisabolol oxide A, generally within the 35–50% range. Its chamazulene content is specified at 2–5%.

**Hungarian type** — Hungarian German Chamomile has a higher α-bisabolol range, specified at 15–40%, and a chamazulene range of 5–22%.

### Does Higher Chamazulene Mean Better Quality?

Not necessarily.

For example, a German Chamomile oil containing 4% chamazulene may be consistent with the Egyptian type, provided its other characteristic constituents also meet the relevant profile.

It would be misleading to classify this oil as inferior simply because its chamazulene content is lower than that of a Hungarian-type oil.

Chamazulene is only one part of the overall chemical profile. For a more meaningful assessment, buyers should consider:

- Chamazulene content.
- α-Bisabolol content.
- α-Bisabolol oxide A and B.
- trans-β-Farnesene.
- The overall chromatographic profile.

The key is to understand the intended chemical type rather than focus on one individual constituent.

## 4. Key Applications of German Chamomile Essential Oil

German Chamomile Essential Oil is widely used in aromatherapy, personal care, and cosmetic formulations. Its characteristic chemical profile makes it an interesting ingredient for products designed around skin conditioning and soothing sensory experiences.

### Skin Care and Personal Care

German Chamomile is particularly well known in skin care formulations.

Its characteristic constituents, including chamazulene, α-bisabolol, and bisabolol oxides, have attracted scientific interest for their biological activities. These compounds have been investigated in experimental studies involving inflammatory pathways and other biological processes.

For cosmetic formulators, German Chamomile Essential Oil may be considered for:

- Skin-conditioning formulations.
- Soothing-themed body oils.
- Massage oil blends.
- Selected personal care products.

However, laboratory findings on isolated compounds or chamomile extracts should not automatically be interpreted as clinical evidence for the essential oil itself.

### Aromatherapy and Fragrance Blending

German Chamomile has a distinctive warm, herbaceous aroma with slightly sweet and earthy nuances. It can be considered in blends with essential oils such as:

- Lavender
- Frankincense
- Clary Sage
- Sweet Orange
- Cedarwood

Its relatively distinctive aroma and blue color can also make it an interesting component in specialty aromatic formulations.

### Traditional Herbal Uses: An Important Distinction

Chamomile flowers have a long history of traditional use in herbal preparations, including teas and other botanical products.

However, chamomile flower tea, botanical extracts, and steam-distilled essential oil are **different materials with different chemical compositions**. Evidence relating to one preparation cannot automatically be applied to another.

German Chamomile Essential Oil should not be presented as an established treatment for digestive disorders or other medical conditions.

## 5. How to Evaluate German Chamomile Essential Oil Quality

For essential oil buyers, a product description and a blue appearance are not enough to establish quality. A more reliable assessment should include botanical identity, chemical composition, physical properties, and batch documentation.

![Blue German Chamomile essential oil](/images/german-chamomile-oil.jpg)

### 1. Confirm the Botanical Identity

Check that the botanical name is clearly stated as:

- ***Matricaria chamomilla L.***

The synonym *Matricaria recutita L.* is also commonly used. This helps distinguish German Chamomile from Roman Chamomile and other products marketed under the general name Chamomile Oil.

### 2. Review the GC-MS Report

GC-MS analysis is an important tool for evaluating the volatile chemical composition of German Chamomile Essential Oil. For this oil, particular attention should be paid to:

- Chamazulene
- α-Bisabolol
- α-Bisabolol oxide A
- α-Bisabolol oxide B
- α-Bisabolone oxide A
- trans-β-Farnesene

Rather than looking at only the chamazulene percentage, buyers should compare the overall chromatographic profile with the relevant ISO reference type. It is also important to check that the GC-MS report corresponds to the actual batch being purchased.

### 3. Check Physical Properties

Physical characteristics provide additional information when evaluating an essential oil. Relevant parameters may include:

- Appearance and color.
- Relative density.
- Refractive index.
- Optical rotation.
- Aroma characteristics.

For reference, ISO 19332:2020 specifies the following relative density ranges at 20°C:

| Parameter | Egyptian type | Hungarian type |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Relative density (20°C)** | 0.910–0.970 | 0.910–0.950 |

These values should be considered together with the chromatographic profile, not as standalone quality indicators.

### 4. Compare Batch Consistency

Natural essential oils can vary because of differences in plant genetics, growing conditions, harvesting periods, and distillation processes.

For buyers purchasing German Chamomile Essential Oil regularly, comparing batch-specific analytical reports can help identify changes in chemical composition over time.

It is useful to discuss the expected chemical profile, specification requirements, and documentation before confirming a bulk order.

## 6. Safety and Handling Considerations

Although German Chamomile Essential Oil is commonly used in personal care and aromatherapy, natural origin does not automatically mean risk-free. Several precautions should be considered:

- **Asteraceae sensitivity** — People with known allergies to chamomile or related plants should exercise particular caution.
- **Skin application** — Do not apply undiluted essential oil directly to the skin. Appropriate formulation and dilution are necessary.
- **Sensitive skin** — A suitable compatibility assessment should be performed before wider application.
- **Pregnancy and children** — Product suitability should be assessed by a qualified professional before use.
- **Storage** — Keep the oil tightly sealed and protected from excessive heat, light, and air exposure to help minimize oxidation and quality changes.

For cosmetic and personal care manufacturers, finished-product safety should be evaluated according to the intended application, concentration, and relevant regulatory requirements.

## 7. Final Thoughts: Understanding the Chemistry Behind the Blue Color

German Chamomile Essential Oil is much more than a blue essential oil.

Its distinctive color comes from chamazulene formed during steam distillation, while its overall chemical profile includes several characteristic compounds such as α-bisabolol, bisabolol oxides, and trans-β-farnesene.

The differences between Egyptian and Hungarian types also show why a single percentage cannot tell the whole story.

For buyers and formulators, understanding botanical identity, chemical composition, GC-MS results, and batch consistency is a much more practical approach to evaluating this natural ingredient.
